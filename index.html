Guide 1 · Bathroom · Cost by state

How much does a walk-in tub cost, installed — and why your state changes the answer

National guides quote one wide range because there is no single price: the tub itself is only part of the bill. Permits, plumbing moves, water-heater capacity and local labour decide where you land.

The short answer: Angi's 2026 data puts walk-in tubs at $1,100 – $17,000 across projects — an estimate range, not a quote. Basic tub swaps in lower-cost labour markets sit near the bottom; jetted models with plumbing relocation, a water-heater upgrade and permit/inspection fees sit at the top, in any state.

Check your state

Pick a state to see the same published range with the state-specific factors that push a quote up or down. No honest source publishes a verified per-state average — anyone who gives you an exact “Florida price” without seeing your bathroom is guessing.

Before you sign

  • Get three itemised quotes. “Installed price” with no breakdown is how a $5,000 job becomes $11,000.
  • Ask whether the quote includes the water-heater check — running out of hot water mid-fill is the most common owner complaint.
  • Read tub vs. shower first: for many households a curbless shower is safer and cheaper.
  • Check Medicare coverage and, for veterans, VA HISA before paying in full.

Guide 2 · Bathroom · Compare

Walk-in tub vs. walk-in shower: the comparison sellers won't give you

Tub companies sell tubs and shower companies sell showers. This comparison sells neither — it walks through the daily-use realities that decide which one a household still likes a year later.

Tub vs. shower, side by side
FactorWalk-in tubWalk-in / curbless shower
Getting inStep over a low threshold, sit, close the door — then waitStep or roll straight in; a wheelchair can enter a curbless design
Fill & drain waitYou sit inside while it fills and fully drains before the door opens — often the deal-breakerNone
Hot waterDeep tubs can drain a small water heaterStandard use
Soaking / hydrotherapyYes — the main reason to choose oneNo soaking; steam and body jets are partial substitutes

Who should not buy a walk-in tub

  • Anyone whose mobility is declining fast — a tub's threshold and transfer get harder; a curbless shower keeps working with a wheelchair.
  • Anyone who dislikes waiting: you cannot open the door until the tub drains.
  • Homes with a small water heater and no budget to upsize it.
  • Households where a caregiver will need to help with bathing.

Who a tub genuinely suits

Someone who loves soaking, has stable mobility, has the hot-water capacity, and understands the fill/drain wait. If that's your parent, a tub can be the right buy — just price it with the checklist in Guide 1.

Guide 3 · Who pays · Medicare

Does Medicare pay for a walk-in tub, grab bars, or a stair lift?

The honest headline first: for these three items, Original Medicare's answer is usually no. But “usually” matters — Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid waivers and VA benefits each have exceptions worth one phone call.

The short answer: Original Medicare generally does not pay for walk-in tubs, grab bars or stair lifts — it treats them as home modifications or convenience items, not durable medical equipment. Some Medicare Advantage plans add home-safety or bathroom-device benefits, some state Medicaid HCBS waivers fund modifications, and the VA covers eligible veterans through HISA and related grants. Check all three before paying out of pocket.
Coverage by payer (general information — confirm with your plan)
ItemOriginal MedicareMedicare AdvantageMedicaid waiver / VA
Walk-in tubUsually noSome plans offer home-safety allowances — plan-specificSome state HCBS waivers fund bathroom modifications; VA HISA may help eligible veterans
Grab barsUsually noSometimes included in supplemental home-safety benefitsOften waiver-eligible as a low-cost safety modification
Stair liftUsually noRarely; check your plan's supplemental benefitsSome waivers and VA grants may contribute — eligibility-based

The three calls to make before you pay

  • Your plan: If you're on Medicare Advantage, call the number on your card and ask specifically about “supplemental benefits for home safety devices and modifications.” Benefits change yearly.
  • Your state Medicaid office / Area Agency on Aging: Ask about Home and Community Based Services (HCBS) waivers and any state home-modification programme.
  • The VA (veterans): Ask about HISA — see Guide 4 — and SAH/SHA housing grants.

Beware any salesperson who says “Medicare will cover it” as a closing tactic and asks you to sign the same day. Legitimate coverage is confirmed by your plan, in writing — never by a vendor.

Not medical or legal advice. Coverage rules change by plan, year and state. Confirm your specific coverage with Medicare (1-800-MEDICARE), your Medicare Advantage plan, your state Medicaid office, or the VA before relying on any statement here.

Sources: Medicare.gov (coverage rules for durable medical equipment and home modifications); VA disability housing grants; research summary in More Opportunities report §1A.

Guide 4 · Who pays · Veterans

VA HISA grants for home modifications: how to apply, step by step

The Home Improvements and Structural Alterations (HISA) grant helps eligible veterans pay for medically necessary home changes — bathroom access, ramps, doorways and more. It is separate from the larger SAH/SHA housing grants.

HISA vs. SAH/SHA — which one?

VA home-modification help (general information)
ProgrammeForScale
HISAMedically necessary improvements — accessible bathroom, ramps, widened doors, counter adjustmentsSmaller, project-level grant; lifetime benefit with different caps for service-connected vs. non-service-connected conditions — check current amounts at VA.gov
SAH (Specially Adapted Housing)Veterans with certain severe service-connected disabilities, to build or adapt a homeLarge grant, set annually
SHA (Special Housing Adaptation)Veterans with certain service-connected disabilities, to adapt a home they own or will ownMid-size grant, set annually

How applying usually works

  • 1. Confirm eligibility: You must be a veteran (or service member) with a prescription or medical determination that the modification is necessary. Both service-connected and non-service-connected conditions can qualify for HISA, at different benefit caps.
  • 2. Get the medical documentation: A VA clinician's statement of medical necessity is the core of the application.
  • 3. Gather contractor quotes: The VA will want itemised quotes showing the work the grant is funding. Vague “bathroom remodel — $12,000” quotes slow applications down.
  • 4. Apply through VA Prosthetics: HISA is administered through the VA health system's Prosthetic and Sensory Aids Service at your VA medical centre.
  • 5. Wait for approval before starting work: Starting construction before approval can make the work ineligible — confirm timing with your VA centre first.

Your state veterans' affairs office can also flag state-level programmes that stack with federal ones. Find yours through VA.gov's state directory.

Not legal or benefits advice. Grant amounts, caps and eligibility are set by the VA and change over time. Confirm current HISA amounts and your eligibility at VA.gov or your VA medical centre before planning around them. All project costs remain estimates, not quotes.

Sources: U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs — disability housing grants (SAH/SHA) and VA Prosthetics HISA programme pages at va.gov.

Tub-to-walk-in-shower conversion: where the money actually goes

This is the most common aging-in-place bathroom job — and the one with the widest quote spread, because “conversion” can mean a one-day panel kit or a full curbless rebuild. Here's how to tell which one you're being quoted.

Guide 6 · Bathroom · How-to

Grab bars: where to place them, at what height — and how to anchor them properly

Grab bars are the cheapest serious safety upgrade in the home — Angi prices them at $100–$500 each installed — but only when they're placed where a falling person actually reaches, and anchored into structure, not drywall.

Placement by location

Grab bar placement (general guidance — ADA-style reference points)
LocationPlacementWhy
Shower entryVertical bar at the entry edgeSupport during the step-in, the highest-risk moment
Shower back wallHorizontal bar, roughly 33–36 inches above the floorSteadying while standing and turning
TubBar at the entry end plus one along the long wallSupport lowering into and rising from the tub
ToiletBar on the side wall beside the toilet, and behind it where the wall allowsLeverage for sitting and standing
Hallways & stairsContinuous railings both sides where possibleFalls aren't only a bathroom problem

The three rules that matter more than the bar itself

  • Anchor into studs or proper blocking. A bar screwed into drywall or tile alone will pull out under a falling person's weight. If studs aren't where the bar needs to be, a contractor can add blocking or use rated anchoring systems.
  • Suction-cup bars are not grab bars. They can release without warning and are suitable, at most, as a light balance touch — never as fall support. If it attaches with a suction cup, don't trust a life to it.
  • Diameter and clearance matter. Bars about 1¼–1½ inches in diameter, mounted roughly 1½ inches off the wall, are the grip standard hands can actually close around.
Estimates, not quotes — and not medical advice. Placement heights are general reference points; an occupational therapist can tailor placement to the person. Confirm installed cost ($100–$500 per bar, Angi 2026) with a local installer, and never rely on a bar that hasn't been anchor-tested.

Sources: Angi (2026) grab bar costs; CDC, fall prevention guidance; AARP HomeFit Guide (grab bar placement).

Guide 7 · Mobility · Cost

Stair lift cost: straight vs. curved, new vs. rental

A straight stair lift is a catalogue product. A curved one is a piece of custom manufacturing. Understanding that difference is the whole pricing story.

The short answer: Angi's 2026 range for stair lifts is $1,500 – $5,000 — that describes most straight installs. A curved lift needs a rail bent to your exact staircase, so expect it to cost substantially more than a straight lift; get the rail quoted as its own line item. All estimates, not quotes.

Straight vs. curved

Stair lift comparison (estimates)
StraightCurved
RailStock rail, cut to lengthCustom-bent to your stairs, measured by survey
Typical cost$1,500 – $5,000 (Angi range)Above the straight range — quote-dependent
Lead timeDaysWeeks (rail fabrication)
Resale / reuseUsed and refurbished market existsRail fits one staircase only; chair may be reusable
RentalCommonly available — good for recovery from surgeryRare and pricey

Rent, buy used, or buy new?

  • Rent when the need is temporary (post-surgery recovery) or you're testing whether the person will use it.
  • Buy used/refurbished for straight stairs only, and only with installation and a warranty from the seller — a lift's safety depends on its fitting.
  • Buy new for curved stairs, heavy use, or when warranty and service coverage matter most.

Measure before anyone quotes

  • Stair width and the user's seated width — narrow stairs may need a folding rail or perch seat.
  • Landings: does a door open onto the top or bottom step? Does the rail block a hallway?
  • Power: lifts run on battery with a charger at one end — confirm outlet placement.
Estimates, not quotes. The $1,500–$5,000 range is Angi's 2026 national range, driven by straight lifts. Curved lifts, outdoor lifts and heavy-capacity models cost more — confirm with a measured, itemised quote. Medicare usually does not pay for stair lifts — see Guide 3. Not medical or legal advice.

Sources: Angi, accessibility costs (2026); Medicare coverage notes in Guide 3.

Guide 8 · Mobility · Cost

Wheelchair ramp cost: wood vs. aluminium vs. modular — and the slope maths that sets the price

Ramp price is mostly length, and length is mostly maths: the height of your step decides how long the ramp must be. Do the maths first and no quote can surprise you.

The slope rule: the standard maximum slope is 1:12 — for every 1 inch of rise, you need 12 inches (1 foot) of ramp. A typical 24-inch-high entry therefore needs about 24 feet of ramp, plus level landings. That length — not the material alone — is what you're paying for.

Work out your length

Rise → ramp length at 1:12 (before landings)
Entry height (rise)Minimum ramp runWhat that means
6 inches (one step)6 feetA portable or threshold ramp may suffice
12 inches12 feetShort modular or wood ramp
24 inches24 feetOften needs a switchback or L-shape
30 inches30 feetSwitchback with a mid-landing is typical

Material choice

Ramp materials compared
MaterialStrengthsWatch out for
Wood (built on site)Lowest build cost; matches the houseMaintenance, slippery when wet, treated as a permanent structure for permits
Modular aluminiumFast install, non-slip surface, removable and resalable, often rentableHigher upfront cost per foot; industrial look
ConcretePermanent, low maintenanceMost expensive to change or remove; permit-heavy
Portable / thresholdCheapest; no permit; good for one low stepOnly for small rises; check weight rating

Permits and HOA rules

Permanent ramps often need a building permit; modular ramps sometimes don't — it varies by city, so ask your building department before building, not after. HOAs may have appearance rules, but accessibility needs carry legal weight in many jurisdictions; if an HOA pushes back, get the rules in writing and consider advice from your Area Agency on Aging or a disability-rights organisation before conceding.

Estimates, not quotes. Ramp totals scale with length, landings and material; anchor your expectations with the accessible-remodel range ($701–$8,041 for most, Angi 2026) and confirm with itemised quotes. Slope figures follow the standard 1:12 accessibility guideline — confirm local code with your building department. Not legal advice.

Guide 9 · Planning & safety

Home safety checklist for parents living alone

The full reasoning behind the walk-through above: why the order matters, and how to have the conversation without it sounding like a takeover.

Why bathroom → stairs → entry

CDC fall-prevention guidance and AARP's HomeFit work point to the same pattern: the bathroom combines water, hard surfaces and transfers (standing from low seats, stepping over tub walls); stairs add height; the entry adds weather, steps and packages in arms. Fixing in that order removes the most risk per dollar first — and the walk-through above is ordered exactly that way, with the cheap fixes ($ tier) deliberately ahead of the remodels.

How to raise it with a parent

  • Lead with a specific, small fix (“let's put a bar by the tub”) not a verdict (“this house isn't safe”).
  • Frame it as staying home longer, not as decline: every item on the list is independence equipment.
  • Do the free fixes together on the first visit — momentum beats a master plan.
  • Agree the check-in routine (call days, spare key, alert device) as a two-way arrangement.
Not medical advice. General information from CDC/AARP guidance; a parent who has fallen, feels dizzy, or takes multiple medications needs a clinician's falls-risk review. Costs shown are estimates, not quotes.

Sources: CDC, Preventing Falls and STEADI materials; AARP HomeFit Guide.

Guide 10 · Planning · Hiring

How to find a CAPS contractor — and the five questions that vet one

CAPS — Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist — is a credential run through the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB). It signals training in designing and building for older adults. It does not, by itself, guarantee a good contractor — so you still vet.

Finding one

  • Start with the NAHB's CAPS directory, then cross-check: state licence, insurance, and recent local references.
  • Your local Area Agency on Aging and independent-living centre often keep informal lists of contractors who do this work well.
  • Occupational therapists who do home assessments are an underused source of contractor referrals — they see whose work actually functions.

Five vetting questions

  • “Show me photos of two aging-in-place jobs like mine, and let me call those clients.”
  • “Where will you put blocking for grab bars we might add later?” — specialists plan for it; generalists look puzzled.
  • “Is your quote itemised — unit, plumbing, electrical, permit, disposal — line by line?”
  • “Who pulls the permit, and will I see the inspection sign-off?”
  • “What would you do differently if this were for your own parent?” — listen for specifics, not slogans.

Red flags

  • High-pressure in-home sales, especially for walk-in tubs and stair lifts: “today-only” pricing, refusal to leave a written quote.
  • Claims that “Medicare will pay for it” — see Guide 3.
  • Large cash deposits before permits, materials or a start date exist.
  • No licence number on the quote, or a licence that doesn't check out with the state.
Estimates, not quotes — and not legal advice. Verify licences and insurance with your state board, get contracts in writing, and confirm any coverage claims with your plan. Credential details are NAHB's; confirm a contractor's current certification in the NAHB directory.

Sources: NAHB, CAPS programme and directory (nahb.org); AARP HomeFit Guide (working with contractors).

Guide 11 · Bathroom · Compare

Comfort-height toilet vs. raised toilet seat: the $200 fix before the $10,000 remodel

Standing up from a low toilet is one of the hardest daily movements for ageing knees and hips. Two fixes exist at very different prices — and the cheaper one is often the right first step.

Toilet options compared
Raised toilet seatComfort-height toilet
What it isA seat that clamps or bolts onto the existing toilet, adding heightA replacement toilet with a taller bowl (about 17–19 inches to the seat, chair height)
InstallMinutes, no plumber, reversible — ideal for recovery from surgeryPlumber or confident DIY; check your rough-in measurement and bowl shape (round vs. elongated) first

The order to do it in

  • Start with a quality bolted raised seat with arms. If it solves the problem, you've spent the least possible.
  • If the need is permanent and the bathroom is due other work, fold a comfort-height toilet into that project — the marginal cost inside a remodel is small.
  • Whichever you choose, add the beside-toilet grab bar (Guide 6) — height plus something to pull on is the real combination.
